\title{The Economics of Protected Rejection:\\
Bonds, Forfeits and Termination Fees\\ in a Permissionless Registry}
\titlerunning{Economics of Protected Rejection}
\author{}
\institute{}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A permissionless registry lets anyone fold a queued request into shared
state, so a request that waits in the queue can be cancelled by a stranger
who collects the fee for the slot.  This paper gives the economic rules of
one such registry.  Every request carries a bond beside its deposit.  A
processor that rejects a request forfeits the bond only when it proves,
against the state root the request recorded at booking, that the request
was already faulty then; any other rejection refunds the bond.  The
forfeited bond is split between the proving processor and the treasury.
A fold that terminates a key donates a termination fee to the treasury.
We state each property as a definition with a theorem, give the transfer
function as one table, derive every parameter constraint as an inequality
with a worked example in lovelace, and list exactly what the mechanism
does not price, with counterexamples.  \notyetmechanized{The Lean model
behind these statements is being built while this draft is reviewed; every
statement below is marked until its proof lands, and nothing is claimed
that the model does not prove.}
\end{abstract}

\keywords{permissionless registry \and transaction fee mechanism \and
bond \and griefing \and front-running \and mechanized proof}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A registry on a public ledger maps keys to owners.  Requests to change
the map wait in a queue, and any party may fold a queued request into
the state.  The folder pays the ledger fee for the slot and is
compensated from the request.  The danger is immediate: a processor
can cancel a request it dislikes and still collect for the slot, so a
requester who booked correctly can lose money to a stranger.

The design studied here answers with money, not with permission.
Folding stays open to everyone.  What changes is what each outcome
pays.  Every request carries two amounts: a deposit, which the request
always gets back, and a bond, which it loses only when a processor
proves a fault.  The proof is checked against the state the request
itself recorded when it was booked, so a fault that arose later ---
because someone else folded first --- costs the requester nothing.
A processor that proves a fault keeps a share of the bond; the rest
goes to the treasury.  A fold that ends the life of a key pays the
treasury a termination fee.

This paper is written for a reader who reviews the registry from the
outside.  It states the whole mechanism on a few pages, proves what
holds, exhibits what does not hold with counterexamples, and derives
the parameter values from the attacks they must deter.  Its claims
are checked against a mechanized model written in Lean.  The model is
being built while this draft is reviewed, so every statement carries
either a Lean name that resolves to a proved theorem or a visible
label saying the proof has not landed yet.  The appendix maps each
statement to its declaration, file, line, commit and axioms.

Section~2 describes the ledger surface the mechanism runs on.
Section~3 fixes who the attacker may be and what each action costs.
Section~4 defines the mechanism and its transfer function.
Section~5 states the properties as definitions with theorems.
Section~6 gives the proofs, mechanized by name or argued by hand and
labelled as such.  Section~7 derives the parameter constraints with
worked numbers.  Section~8 places the work among verified prior
results.  Section~9 lists the limitations.  The appendix carries the
artifact table.

\section{System model}
\label{sec:system}

The ledger follows the extended unspent transaction output model: state
is held in unspent outputs, and a transaction spends some outputs and
creates others under the checks of the scripts that guard them.  The
registry keeps one state output holding the map from keys to leaves,
and commits to that map with a trie root stored beside it.  We write
$R$ for a state and $\mathrm{root}(R)$ for its root.  A reader who
holds the root can verify any leaf presented with its path; tags on
commitments are assumed collision free, which the appendix records as
an explicit assumption about the hash function in use.

A request names an edge of the registry law --- the registry admits two,
registering a free key and terminating a live key; every other edge
of the full law is excluded and its request is refused at once ---
together with the key, the owner, the destination that should receive
the token, a deposit $D$, a bond $B$, a tip $\tau$ and the root the
requester read at booking, written $\rho_{b}$.  The request lives in
its own output until it leaves the queue.  Booking is not a state
transition of the model: a request is an argument the law is asked
about, and the booking rule is a defined admission check.  The check
requires that the bond carried is at least the registry bond, that a
request to terminate carries at least the registry fee, and that the
recorded root equal the root of the state read at booking.  The
request-tag mint must enforce exactly this admission, and every
honesty statement quantifies only over requests that passed it.

Time is measured in milliseconds of ledger time.  Each request carries its booked submission
time.  Two windows govern its life.  The processing window of length
$W_{p}$ is the time during which a processor should fold it.  The
reclaim window of length $W_{r}$ follows, during which the owner may
take the request back.  A request is expired when the slot reaches the
booked time plus $W_{p}$ plus $W_{r}$.  Folds are serialized: the law
judges each action against the state left by the preceding actions in
the same ordered batch, and rejection evidence binds that intermediate
state and its root.  Each fold carries at most a bounded number of
requests, written $k$ for the capacity of one fold.  A batch of one
behaves exactly as the single action does, so the batch form adds
no new outcome, only the state threading between consecutive
actions.

A transaction that settles an outcome moves lovelace between outputs.
The model states floors, not exact payments: what the owner must
receive at least, what the treasury must receive at least, and what
the processor can keep at most.  Conservation is stated for a
balanced transaction, one whose inputs equal its outputs plus the
donation plus the ledger fee; the balance is a named premise of the
theorem, not a rule of the model, because the model has no ledger
fee.  Amounts are integers in lovelace.  The processor share of a
forfeited bond is the floor of the split times the bond, and the
treasury receives the remainder, so the two shares add up to the bond
exactly.  A donation the ledger owes is written in the donation field
of the transaction.  That field accepts only positive amounts, so
when the owed amount is zero the field is omitted and no donation is
owed; the obligation applies only to positive amounts.

\section{Adversary and cost model}
\label{sec:adversary}

Four roles appear: the requester who books, the processor who folds
or rejects, the block producer who orders transactions, and the
treasury, which receives donations under the rules of the ledger and
spends them under the rules of governance.  Any roles may coincide:
a processor may book its own requests, a block producer may run a
processor, and treasury voters may produce blocks.  Each coincidence
is bounded separately or declared out of scope in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The attacker controls block production for the slots it wins, sees
the mempool of pending transactions, and orders transactions within
its blocks, including inserting its own before or around the
transactions of others.  It cannot forge signatures, cannot rewrite
the booked submission time of a request it did not create, and cannot
present a leaf path that does not verify against a committed root,
by the collision-freedom assumption.

Requesters in the honesty statements are not strategic: an honest
requester books a well formed request against a state in which its
edge is admissible and then waits.  Processors and producers are
rational in the narrow sense used throughout: each prefers more
lovelace to less and will take any action the law admits that
increases its balance.  The analysis does not assume that attackers
are few, coordinated, or computationally bounded beyond the signature
and hash assumptions.

Each action costs real money.  Booking locks the deposit and the
bond as capital for the life of the request and pays a ledger fee
$c_{\mathrm{book}}$.  Folding pays $c_{\mathrm{fold}}$.  Rejecting
pays $c_{\mathrm{rej}}$.  Retracting pays $c_{\mathrm{ret}}$.
Table~\ref{tab:costs} collects who pays what.  Capital locked for a
duration carries an opportunity cost at the
prevailing rate $r$, so a request that lives through both windows
costs its holder $r(D + B)(W_{p} + W_{r})$ in forgone return besides
any amount it finally loses.

\section{Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The registry creator sets three parameters at creation, and no fold
ever changes them: the bond $B$ every request carries beside its
deposit, the processor split $\sigma = n/d$, a numerator over a
denominator and strictly below one, giving the processor share of a
forfeited bond, and the termination fee $F$ a terminating fold
donates per terminated key.  The requester sets the tip $\tau$ per
request, which pays the processor for the slot.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}

\midrule

A request leaves the queue in one of five ways.  It is folded; the
owner takes it back inside the reclaim window, called a retraction;
or a processor rejects it on one of two grounds with a chosen proof.
The two grounds are a lifecycle mismatch --- the request edge has no
successor from the leaf its key holds --- and expiry.  A rejection
that claims the forfeit must carry the whole logical map whose
commitment is the recorded booking root $\rho_{b}$; the model checks
that the commitment of the supplied map equals $\rho_{b}$ and that
the edge has no successor from the leaf the supplied map holds at
the key.  This is the authenticated logical read the rejection
evidence already uses, one state earlier.  The processor chooses the
ground: a rejection on the expiry ground never forfeits, and a
rejection on the mismatch ground forfeits exactly when its fault
proof holds.  A mismatch that arose after booking has no fault
proof, so the requester keeps the bond.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} is the transfer function.  Each row gives
the net effect per party, excluding the key value, with ledger fees
shown where they fall.  The owner's deposit and approval are refunded
on every rejection; the tip is returned on a retraction and paid to
the processor on everything else, so an honest requester whose
request is rejected without proof or on expiry loses the tip.  The
fee of a termination is owed per terminated key, so a batch of
terminations owes the fee times their number.

Three readings of the table matter.  First, the honest requester
loses no deposit and no bond in any outcome; the worst case before
fees is minus the tip.  Second, the attacker that books a faulty
request and rejects it through its own processor still loses the
treasury share $B - \lfloor\sigma B\rfloor$ plus both fees, because
the tip and the processor share round-trip to itself.  Third, the
tip buys the slot and nothing else: on a retraction the processor
receives nothing, since no processor spent a slot.

\section{Properties}
\label{sec:properties}

Each property is a definition with a theorem.  The Lean name follows
in monospace through the \lean{} macro so it can be checked; while
the model is being built every claim is wrapped in the visible
label.  Statements quantify over states reachable from genesis by
folds, and say so; over an arbitrary state value the supply and
honesty claims are simply false.

\begin{definition}[Booking admission]
A request is admitted when its bond is at least the registry bond, a
terminate request carries at least the registry fee, and its recorded
root equals the root of the state read at booking.  Registry
parameters are preserved by every fold.
\notyetmechanized{Mechanized as \lean{parameters\_preserved\_by\_fold}.}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Parameters never move]
\notyetmechanized{The bond, the split and the termination fee are
equal before and after every fold: \lean{parameters\_preserved\_by\_fold}.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Transfer conservation]
In every outcome the owner floor, the treasury floor and the
processor ceiling add up to what the request held, for a balanced
transaction.  The processor share plus the treasury share equals the
bond exactly, and with the split below one and a positive bond the
treasury share is positive.  A zero owed amount imposes no donation,
so the field can be omitted.
\notyetmechanized{Mechanized as \lean{transfer\_conserves},
\lean{forfeit\_shares\_add\_up}, \lean{treasury\_share\_positive} and
\lean{zero\_owed\_means\_no\_donation}.}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Every outcome conserves]
\notyetmechanized{In every outcome the three amounts of
Table~\ref{tab:transfer} sum to the request holdings:
\lean{transfer\_conserves}.  The two forfeit shares sum to the bond:
\lean{forfeit\_shares\_add\_up}.  The treasury share of a positive
bond is positive when the split is below one:
\lean{treasury\_share\_positive}.  Zero owed means no donation field:
\lean{zero\_owed\_means\_no\_donation}.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Fault at booking]
A fault is a lifecycle mismatch at booking: the request edge has no
successor from the leaf its key held in the state with the recorded
root, including a request that names an excluded edge.  A key that is
known never becomes unknown, so a fault at booking of a registration
persists as a mismatch.
\notyetmechanized{Mechanized as \lean{forfeit\_requires\_fault\_at\_booking}
with \lean{known\_stays\_known}.}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Forfeit needs a proved fault]
\notyetmechanized{A rejection that forfeits carries a map whose
commitment is the recorded booking root and in which the edge has no
successor at the key: \lean{forfeit\_requires\_fault\_at\_booking}.
A rejection without a valid booking proof owes the owner deposit and
bond: \lean{no\_proof\_no\_forfeit}.  A rejection on the expiry
ground owes the owner deposit and bond:
\lean{expiry\_never\_forfeits}.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Honest request]
A request is honest when it passed the booking admission against a
reachable state in which its edge was admissible, under
collision-free commitments.  No live request is rejected before
expiry: a request that can be folded now cannot be rejected until
its expiry.
\notyetmechanized{Mechanized as \lean{honest\_requester\_keeps\_bond}
with \lean{live\_compatible\_request\_protected}.}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[The honest requester keeps deposit and bond]
\notyetmechanized{If the request passed the booking admission against
a reachable state in which its edge was admissible, and commitments
are collision free, then no reachable state and no admitted rejection
takes its deposit or its bond: \lean{honest\_requester\_keeps\_bond}.
Its lovelace outcome is at least minus the tip before fees:
\lean{honest\_payoff\_floor}.  This is safety, not liveness: the tip
is lost on expiry and on a lost race, and delay is not bounded.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Unproved rejections pay the processor at most the tip]
\notyetmechanized{On a rejection without proof the processor keeps at
most the tip: \lean{rejection\_without\_proof\_processor\_cap}.  A
fold and a retraction owe the owner the bond, and a retraction owes
deposit, tip and bond: \lean{fold\_returns\_bond} and
\lean{retraction\_returns\_everything}.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Self-rejection and termination]
A self-rejection is a faulty request whose requester is also the
rejecting processor.  Termination ends a key as terminal and owes
the treasury at least the fee per terminated key, also in a batch.
\notyetmechanized{Mechanized as \lean{self\_rejection\_cost} and
\lean{termination\_cost\_floor} with \lean{termination\_donates\_fee}.}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Attack floors]
\notyetmechanized{A requester that is also the processor of its own
faulty request loses at least the treasury share, when the split is
below one: \lean{self\_rejection\_cost}.  Ending a key as terminal
costs at least the fee; the deposit comes back, so the deposit is
capital and the fee is the cost: \lean{termination\_cost\_floor}.  A
fold that terminates a key owes the treasury at least the fee per
terminated key: \lean{termination\_donates\_fee}.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Anyone may reject; the owner may leave late]
\notyetmechanized{A rejection requires no signer, so anyone may
submit it, the owner of an expired request included:
\lean{rejection\_requires\_no\_signer}.  After the reclaim window
the owner of a request exits by rejecting it itself on the expiry
ground: deposit and bond come back to it, and it pays only fees:
\lean{owner\_exit\_after\_expiry}.  Capital stays locked until
someone does.}
\end{theorem}

The following are hand-derived from the mechanized transfer lemmas.
They involve the key value, opportunity cost or fee volatility, which
the model does not contain, and are labelled as hand arguments in
Section~\ref{sec:proofs}.

\begin{theorem}[Flood cost, griefing bound, incentives, collusion; hand]
A self-rejecting attacker and processor pair pays at least
$(B - \lfloor\sigma B\rfloor) + c_{\mathrm{book}} + c_{\mathrm{rej}}$
per doomed request, since the tip and the processor share
round-trip.  Forcing honest requests to expire needs about
$(W_{p}$ over the slot interval$)$ times $k$ doomed requests.
With victim loss from forced expiries and delay, the griefing
factor, victim loss over attacker cost, stays below one exactly
when the parameters satisfy the inequality of
Section~\ref{sec:sizing}.  Processors clear faults rather than let
them rot when the tip floor plus the processor share covers a
rejection and the tip floor covers a fold share.  Requester and
processor in collusion recover the processor share, so only
$(1 - \sigma)B$ per request is real cost; producer and processor in
collusion is the front-running bound below.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Front-running bound; hand]
A front-runner that registers the key first gains at most the
victim tip when it also processes, and pays deposit, tip and fees
of its own.  The mechanism bounds the victim monetary loss by the
honesty theorem; it does not stop the capture of the key.  The
defence against theft is the approval: admission binds the edge,
the key, the owner and the destination under the pinned application
policy.  Whether the application approves a key only for that key
controller, as with a self-certifying prefix, is an assumption
stated in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, and nothing about the
bond implies it.
\end{theorem}

Four statements do not hold as defences, and the model keeps each
counterexample.  Concurrent bookings of one untaken key are all
admissible at the booking root; the losers cost their tip, not the
bond (\lean{duplicate\_bookings\_cost\_only\_tip}).  With the split
equal to one a self-rejecting requester pays nothing, which is why
the split must stay below one
(\lean{full\_processor\_split\_costs\_nothing}).  A requester that
lost a race loses its tip, fixing the exact scope of the honesty
theorem (\lean{honest\_loses\_tip\_on\_race}).  A front-runner that
registers the key first is not charged for the capture
(\lean{key\_capture\_not\_priced}).

\section{Proofs}
\label{sec:proofs}

\notyetmechanized{No proof below is mechanized yet.  Each item names
the Lean declaration that will carry it, so a reader can check every
name once the model lands.  ``Mechanized'' means a Lean theorem
with only the standard axioms; ``hand'' means derived by hand from
proved lemmas and labelled so, because it speaks of the key value,
fees or opportunity cost, which the model does not contain.}

Mechanized, by name.  Conservation and the share arithmetic
(\lean{transfer\_conserves}, \lean{forfeit\_shares\_add\_up},
\lean{treasury\_share\_positive}, \lean{zero\_owed\_means\_no\_donation})
follow from the transfer definition by integer arithmetic on the
floors.  Preservation (\lean{parameters\_preserved\_by\_fold}) is an
induction over folds.  The fault statements
(\lean{forfeit\_requires\_fault\_at\_booking},
\lean{no\_proof\_no\_forfeit}, \lean{known\_stays\_known},
\lean{expiry\_never\_forfeits}) unfold the rejection evidence and
the two grounds.  Honesty (\lean{honest\_requester\_keeps\_bond},
\lean{honest\_payoff\_floor},
\lean{live\_compatible\_request\_protected},
\lean{rejection\_without\_proof\_processor\_cap},
\lean{fold\_returns\_bond},
\lean{retraction\_returns\_everything}) combines admission,
reachability and the transfer floors.  Attack floors
(\lean{self\_rejection\_cost}, \lean{termination\_cost\_floor},
\lean{termination\_donates\_fee}) read the forfeit and donation
obligations.  Exit (\lean{rejection\_requires\_no\_signer},
\lean{owner\_exit\_after\_expiry}) follows from the empty signer
list with the expiry ground.  The kept counterexamples
(\lean{duplicate\_bookings\_cost\_only\_tip},
\lean{full\_processor\_split\_costs\_nothing},
\lean{honest\_loses\_tip\_on\_race},
\lean{key\_capture\_not\_priced}) are named theorems stating that
the exhibited scenarios exist with the stated costs.

Hand arguments, labelled.  The per-request flood cost instantiates
the transfer row for a proved fault with requester and processor
the same party: the tip and the processor share cancel, leaving the
treasury share plus the two fees.  The griefing bound divides the
victim loss, forced expiries times tip plus delay cost, by that
per-request cost.  Processor incentive compares the tip floor plus
the processor share against the rejection fee, and the tip floor
against a fold share.  Collusion bounds re-read the same rows with
coinciding roles.  The front-running bound compares the front-run
gain, key value plus victim tip, against the honesty floor for the
victim monetary loss, and stops there: the key itself is outside
the priced surface.

\section{Parameter sizing}
\label{sec:sizing}

Every number below is derived from a named definition, with a worked
example in lovelace.  The running example uses a deposit of
2,000,000, a bond of 5,000,000, a split of one half so the processor
share is 2,500,000 and the treasury share is 2,500,000, a tip of
200,000, a termination fee of 10,000,000, and ledger fees of
170,000 per action.  Capital figures use the prevailing rate $r$
without fixing it.

The split stays below one.  With the split equal to one the
treasury share is zero and a self-rejecting requester pays nothing
but fees, by \lean{full\_processor\_split\_costs\_nothing}.  Hence
$\sigma < 1$, equivalently $n < d$.  In the example one half leaves
2,500,000 of every forfeited bond to the treasury.

The treasury share must exceed the per-request loss a flood is meant
to cost its author.  Write $C$ for that target.  The constraint reads
$B - \lfloor\sigma B\rfloor \geq C$, read from the forfeit row.
With the example numbers the treasury share is 2,500,000, so any
flood author pays at least 2,500,000 plus 340,000 in fees per doomed
request.  A flood that fills every fold of the processing window
needs about $(W_{p}$ over the slot interval$)$ times $k$ such
requests, each priced the same.  Concretely, with a window covering
one hundred folds of ten requests, the flood needs about one
thousand doomed requests, about 2.5 billion lovelace in forfeits
plus fees, to force the honest requests of that window to expire.
The price scales linearly in the window length and the fold
capacity, so lengthening the processing window or widening folds
raises the attacker bill without moving any other parameter.

The processor share plus the tip must cover a rejection, or faults
wait for expiry.  The constraint reads
$\tau_{\min} + \lfloor\sigma B\rfloor \geq c_{\mathrm{rej}}$, read
from the proved-fault row.  In the example 200,000 plus 2,500,000
covers 170,000 with room to spare.  The tip alone must cover a
processor share of a fold: $\tau_{\min} \geq c_{\mathrm{fold}}/k$,
so with ten requests per fold the example tip covers a 17,000 share
ten times over.  The ordering this creates is stated openly:
because a proved fault pays a bounty on top of the tip, processors
clear faults before honest requests.  The bounty is the point ---
faults should clear fast --- and its side effect is that honest
requests wait behind fault clearing when folds are full.

The griefing factor stays below one when the victim loss per forced
expiry, tip plus delay cost, does not exceed the attacker cost per
doomed request, treasury share plus fees.  With the example numbers
a victim loses 200,000 plus delay per expiry while the attacker
pays 2,840,000 per doomed request, so the factor is well below one
before delay is even counted.  Raise the tip or lower the bond and
the inequality tells exactly when the bound breaks.

The termination fee is deliberately expensive, and its lower bound
is derived from what it must deter.  Squat and terminate --- book a
key to deny it to others, then end it --- must cost more than the
value of holding the key terminal.  Write $V_{\mathrm{hold}}$ for
that value.  The constraint reads $F \geq V_{\mathrm{hold}}$, read
from \lean{termination\_cost\_floor}, with the deposit excluded on
purpose: the deposit comes back at termination, so it is capital,
not cost, and only the fee deters.  In the example the fee of
10,000,000 prices out any squat worth less than that to hold.  An
honest owner pays the fee once, at the end of a key life, when the
key is genuinely retired; that is the intended incidence, and the
paper says so plainly.  The contract enforces no floor on the fee:
the booking admission checks that a terminate request carries at
least the registry fee, and the donation obligation settles the
rest.  A zero fee would mean the field is omitted and nothing is
owed, which is why a registry that wants the deterrent sets a
positive fee at creation and can never raise it later.

Capital lockup enters every honest and attacker cost alike:
$r(D + B)(W_{p} + W_{r})$ for a request that lives through both
windows.  In the example 7,000,000 locked through both windows at
any positive rate is the price of patience and the price of delay
alike.  Parameters are fixed at creation in lovelace while key
values move in fiat, so each inequality above carries a safe price
range: the range of native asset prices over which the lovelace
numbers still satisfy the bound.  With immutable parameters the
range is published with the values, not assumed.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Fee mechanism design asks what miners and users will do, not only
what the rules say.  Roughgarden studies transaction fee design as
mechanism design~\cite{Roughgarden21}.  Chung and Shi give
foundations including side-contract proofness, the collusion notion
this paper uses for coinciding roles~\cite{ChungShi23}.  Daian and
coauthors exhibit front-running, extractable value and consensus
instability in decentralized exchanges~\cite{FlashBoys20}.
Eskandari and coauthors systematize front-running attacks on
blockchains~\cite{Eskandari19}.  Qin and coauthors quantify how
much extractable value is taken in practice~\cite{Qin22}.  Babel
and coauthors automate the analysis of economic security in smart
contracts~\cite{Clockwork23}.  Buterin defines discouragement
attacks and the griefing factor, victim loss over attacker
cost~\cite{Buterin18}, which Section~\ref{sec:sizing} bounds.

Capital lockup as a deterrent is studied by Harz and coauthors, who
adjust cryptocurrency deposits dynamically~\cite{Balance19}; the
registry here fixes its amounts at creation instead.  Bonded
challenges appear in Arbitrum~\cite{Arbitrum18}.  Squatting is as
old as decentralized names: Kalodner and coauthors draw lessons
from Namecoin~\cite{Namecoin15}, Johnson specifies the Ethereum
name service~\cite{EIP137}.  Treasury withdrawals
on this ledger follow the Conway governance design~\cite{CIP1694},
which does not mention donations; the donation field the forfeit
and the termination fee use is a separate transaction field that
accepts only positive amounts.

The ledger surface is the extended unspent transaction output
model of Chakravarty and coauthors~\cite{ExtendedUTXO20}.  The
mechanized statements are written in Lean~\cite{Lean4}, and the
closest mechanized ledger precedent is the Agda specification of
the Cardano ledger~\cite{FMBC24}.  Contract specification and
verification at large are surveyed by Tolmach and
coauthors~\cite{Survey21}.  What this paper adds to that record is
the combination the registry needs: a bond whose forfeit is
conditioned on a proved fault against the booking root, a split of
the forfeit between prover and treasury, and a priced termination,
all stated as definitions with theorems and checked against one
mechanized model rather than argued in prose alone.  Two candidates were excluded from this
section on purpose: a Handshake whitepaper that could not be
located is not cited, and a griefing-factors survey whose author
name is unconfirmed is not cited until its author is confirmed.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Every property that does not hold is listed here with its
counterexample, followed by the design facts the mechanism leaves
unpriced and the hazards around the model.

Concurrent duplicate bookings of one untaken key are all admissible
at the booking root, because each copy is valid against the state
its requester read.  The copies that lose the race are rejected
without proof and cost their tip plus fees, not the bond, by
\lean{duplicate\_bookings\_cost\_only\_tip}.  No bond prices this
race; proving that bookings exclude pending keys is open work.

Holding a squatted key without terminating it is not priced by the
termination fee.  The fee fires on termination, and a squatter who
never terminates pays no fee.  The real defence against key theft
is the application approval policy: whether the application
approves a key only for that key controller is an assumption the
honesty statements rest on, and a registry whose application
approves carelessly is not protected by any amount in this paper.

A zero donation cannot be expressed: the donation field accepts
only positive amounts, so a zero treasury share or a zero
termination fee omits the field, by
\lean{zero\_owed\_means\_no\_donation}.  This is an encoding fact,
not a policy choice, and the model owes nothing when nothing is
owed.

The tip fate on a retraction is fixed: the tip returns to the
owner with the deposit and the bond, and the processor receives
nothing, by \lean{retraction\_returns\_everything}.  On every
other outcome the tip pays the processor for the slot.  An honest
requester therefore loses the tip on expiry and on a lost race,
which is the exact scope of the honesty theorem and is not called
``no honest loss'' anywhere in this paper.

Key capture is not priced: a front-runner that registers the key
first is not charged for the capture, by
\lean{key\_capture\_not\_priced}.  The mechanism bounds the victim
monetary loss and not the theft of the key.  Collusion bounds are
per request: requester and processor together recover the
processor share, so only $(1 - \sigma)B$ is real cost per faulty
request.  Producer and treasury-voter coincidences are declared
out of scope.

Liveness is not proved.  State-output contention can delay any
fold; the treasury donation field is checked against the live
treasury when present, which is a liveness hazard at epoch
boundaries; zero-donation cases omit the field and must be handled
as absent, not as zero.  Delay by refusing to process or reject
costs the attacker nothing in this model besides the capital it
chose not to lock; what it costs the honest requester is locked
capital and time.  Fee and price volatility are outside the model:
parameters are fixed in lovelace at creation, key values move
elsewhere, and the safe price range of Section~\ref{sec:sizing} is
a hand computation, not a theorem.
